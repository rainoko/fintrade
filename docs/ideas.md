- **New technique, not currently in this app at all** — Elder ch. 41 "Channel Trading Systems" (*The New Trading for a Living*, 2014, pp. 166-172), a stand-alone method he explicitly says can be *combined with* Triple Screen rather than replacing anything in it:
  - **Symmetrical % channel** around the slower EMA (e.g. 26-day): `Upper = EMA + coef·EMA`, `Lower = EMA − coef·EMA`, coefficient tuned (start 3-5%) so the channel contains ~95% of recent price action. The zone between the fast and slow EMA is the "value zone."
  - **Contrarian rule**: buy near the lower channel line, sell/take-profit near the upper — fade breakouts rather than chase them (opposite mentality to a momentum breakout entry).
  - **Concrete combo signal** (Manning Stoller, p. 170): sell = price at upper channel line **+ a bearish MACD-Histogram divergence** at the same time; buy = price at lower channel line **+ a bullish MACD-Histogram divergence**. Specified precisely enough to actually implement/backtest.
  - Also: ATR-based multi-channel bands (1/2/3-ATR from the MA, Kerry Lovvorn's method) and Bollinger/std-deviation channels (Elder himself prefers plain % channels over Bollinger for stock/futures profit targets, since Bollinger's width constantly moves).
  - Most relevant tie-in for this app: **profit target / stop placement**. Ch. 38 already said "the value zone on a weekly chart presents a good target" for Triple Screen trades — the channel formalizes exactly what "value zone" means, and the current stop-loss logic (`docs/Analyse.md` §7, swing-low minus volatility buffer) doesn't reference it at all. Worth deciding whether channels become (a) a profit-target/stop input, (b) an additional confidence-scoring signal via the divergence-combo rule, or (c) both, or neither for now.

- **Real gap found while reading ch. 51 "The Six Percent Rule"** (pp. 208-210) and cross-checking `backend/app/portfolio/risk.py`: the book's 6% Rule formula has two parts — *"the sum of your losses for the current month **and** the risks in open trades"* (his own worked example, p. 208-209, step 1 = realized losses this month, step 2 = open-position risk, step 3 = sum). This app's `total_open_risk_pct` only ever implements step 2 — it sums risk across currently-open positions and never accounts for realized losses from trades already closed earlier in the same calendar month, because there's no trade-history/ledger model in the schema at all (`app/db/models.py` has `PositionORM`/`AccountORM`/`WatchlistItemORM`/`OHLCVCacheORM`, nothing recording a closed trade's P&L or close date). Concretely: a user who took three straight stopped-out losses earlier this month, then opens a new, individually-fine 2%-sized position, sails right past the 6% Rule today — the app can't see those prior losses.
  - What this would need: a closed-trade/trade-history table (ticker, entry/exit price+date, quantity, realized P&L at minimum), a way to detect a position closing (currently — need to check whether `DELETE /api/portfolio/positions/{id}` or similar already exists and whether it could hook into recording a closed-trade row), and `total_open_risk_pct` (or a new sibling function) summing realized-losses-this-month + open-risk, gated to the current calendar month.
  - Not flagging (already a deliberate, documented decision, not a gap): the book measures equity at the start of each month; this app intentionally uses live current equity throughout instead — `position_risk_pct`'s own docstring explains this was a considered choice already, not something newly found here.

- **Missing entirely: divergence detection.** Confirmed via `grep -ri divergence` across `app/` and `docs/` — zero hits anywhere in this codebase. Elder brings this up repeatedly and treats it as one of his strongest signals: ch. 15 ("bullish/bearish divergences...mark some of the best buying/shorting opportunities"), ch. 18 ("true breakouts are confirmed when indicators reach new extremes...false breakouts are often marked by divergences between prices and indicators"), ch. 39's own Tide example chart ("the uptrend is very strong because signal E came from a bullish divergence"), and ch. 41's Manning Stoller channel+divergence combo (sell = price at upper channel + bearish MACD-Histogram divergence; buy = price at lower channel + bullish divergence). Definition: price makes a new high/low but the indicator (MACD-Histogram is his usual choice) makes a *shallower* extreme than its previous one — the trend is losing momentum even though price hasn't turned yet.
  - Implementation shape: detect on `macd_histogram` (already computed) — compare the latest swing extreme in price against the indicator's value at the prior comparable swing extreme. Could become (a) an input to confidence scoring (a BUY/SELL that also has a supporting divergence is stronger), (b) a standalone early-warning flag on held positions ("bearish divergence forming — trend may be weakening" even while still nominally in gear), or (c) a component of the false-breakout detector below. Needs a swing-high/swing-low detector as a prerequisite, which doesn't exist in the codebase yet either.
  - **Update — exact algorithm now confirmed from ch. 23 "MACD Lines and MACD-Histogram" (pp. 86-89), not just the general concept**, so this is now genuinely implementable, not just a direction:
    - **Bullish divergence** (buy signal): price falls to a new low while MACD-H also falls to a new low → MACD-H must then rally **above its zero/centerline** (this crossover is, in the book's own words, "an absolute must for a true divergence" — no crossover means no divergence, don't call it one) → price falls again to a *lower* low, but MACD-H this time traces a *shallower* low than its first one. The buy signal fires the moment MACD-H, still below zero, simply ticks up (stops declining) — it does **not** need to cross the centerline a second time.
    - **Bearish divergence** (sell signal): exact mirror — MACD-H must cross **below** zero between the two price/indicator tops; sell signal fires when MACD-H ticks down from its second, shallower top, also without needing to re-cross the centerline.
    - **Kerry Lovvorn's empirical refinement** (cited directly, from his own research): the most tradable divergences have their two tops/bottoms **20-40 bars apart, closer to 20 is better** (in whatever timeframe — 20 weeks on a weekly chart, 20 days on a daily chart), and the best ones have the second extreme **no more than half the height/depth of the first**. Both are simple, checkable numeric filters.
    - **Triple divergences** (3 tops or 3 bottoms, even stronger than a double) occur specifically when a regular divergence "aborts" first — the third extreme need only be shallower than the first, not necessarily the second.
    - **"Hound of the Baskervilles" signal** (named for the Sherlock Holmes story — the dog that *didn't* bark was the clue): when a valid divergence forms but price does the *opposite* of what it implies (keeps falling despite a bullish divergence, or keeps rising despite a bearish one), that's not a failed signal to discard — it's a strong continuation signal in the "wrong" direction, because it means something is fundamentally changing beneath the surface. Elder's own explicit rule: on an aborted bearish divergence, go long; on an aborted bullish divergence, go short. This is the one case where he uses a stop-and-reverse.
    - **Stochastic (ch. 26, pp. 97-98) and RSI (ch. 27, pp. 101-102) each get the exact same divergence treatment**, slightly simpler — no centerline-crossing requirement, just directly compare the second bottom/top against the first; strongest when the first extreme is beyond the oscillator's own overbought/oversold reference line and the second is back inside it. Since Stochastic is already computed in this app (`app/indicators/stochastic.py`), this is actually the cheapest of the three divergence types to add first.

- **Missing entirely: horizontal support/resistance.** Ch. 18 "Support and Resistance" (pp. 55-60) — currently zero support/resistance logic anywhere in the app (Screen 3/Trigger only ever compares against *yesterday's* single high/low, not a zone built from multiple touches). Concrete, well-specified rules from the book:
  - A support/resistance zone is a horizontal congestion area (not a single price) — the edges of where price repeatedly stalled, not the extreme wick.
  - Strength scored by three factors: **length** (2 weeks = minor, 2 months = intermediate, 2 years = major), **height** as % of current price (~1% = minor, ~3% = intermediate, ≥7% = major), and **volume traded** within the zone. He gives an explicit dollar-strength formula: days-in-zone × average daily volume × average price.
  - Zones **flip roles** once broken (old resistance becomes new support, and vice versa) — an already-known zone shouldn't be discarded after a breakout, its role just inverts.
  - **False breakout = a specific, high-value trade setup**: price closes beyond support/resistance, then closes back inside the zone — this signals the breakout attempt failed and the reversal is often sharp. Stop placement is explicit: near the false breakout's own extreme (the tip of the failed move), not further out. True breakouts are distinguished by heavy volume + new indicator extremes; false ones by light volume (and divergence, per the idea above).
  - Ties directly into existing app logic: could tighten `docs/Analyse.md` §7's stop-loss calculation when price nears a known S/R zone (book's own trading rule #1: "tighten your protective stop" near support/resistance), and a false-breakout-from-S/R detector could become a new signal type independent of (or feeding into) the existing Trigger screen.

- **Missing entirely: trend vs. trading-range regime detection, and dual trading tactics.** Ch. 19 "Trends and Trading Ranges" (pp. 60-64). Today the app implicitly treats "Tide NEUTRAL" as "nothing to do here" (matches the earlier ORCL/V conversation), but Elder's actual framing is different: **markets spend most of their time in trading ranges, not trends** — a Neutral/ranging market isn't a data gap, it's the normal state, and it has its own tactic (buy weakness/sell strength near range edges) that's the *opposite* of trend tactic (buy strength/sell weakness, follow the breakout). Concrete regime-detection rule he gives: "if a moving average has not reached a new high or low in a month, the market is probably in a trading range" — simple and directly computable from the existing EMA13/26 series. Also: stop width should differ by regime (wider stops trend-following, tighter stops range-trading) — the app's current stop-loss formula (swing-low minus volatility buffer) doesn't vary by regime at all.
  - This is a bigger, more speculative idea than the others above (a real second trading mode, not just a new indicator) — worth a explicit product decision before scoping: should the app ever suggest a range-bound "buy near support / sell near resistance" trade during a Neutral Tide, or does it stay strictly BUY/SELL/HOLD-via-Triple-Screen-only by design? Needs a decision, not just an implementation.

- **New pattern, well-specified: Kangaroo Tails** (ch. 20, pp. 65-67), a.k.a. "fingers" — a 3-bar candlestick reversal pattern, completely separate from anything currently in the codebase (no candlestick-pattern detection exists at all). Definition: a single bar with range ~2-3x the recent average bar height, protruding from a tight recent range, where the close ends up back near the open (not at the extreme) — flanked by two bars of normal height. An upward-pointing tail (new high, closes back down) = bearish reversal signal; a downward-pointing tail (new low, closes back up) = bullish reversal signal. Detectable purely from OHLC — no new indicator needed, just a bar-range-vs-recent-average-range comparison plus a confirming next bar. Stop placement is explicit: halfway through the tail, not at its tip (too wide) or at its base (too tight). Could be implemented as a standalone alert/signal independent of Triple Screen, similar in spirit to the false-breakout idea above.

- **Missing entirely: the Directional System / ADX.** Ch. 24 (pp. 89-93). Confirmed via `grep` — no `true_range`, `atr`, `directional_system`, or `adx` anywhere in `app/`. Elder calls this out repeatedly (ch. 19's trend-identification checklist lists it, ch. 39 references it) as the best tool specifically for catching *the start* of a new trend, which is exactly the gap the trend-vs-range regime idea above needs a real mechanism for, rather than just the rough "EMA hasn't made a new high/low in a month" heuristic. Fully specified, computable from OHLC alone:
  - `+DM`/`-DM`: the portion of today's high-low range that extends beyond yesterday's range, signed by direction.
  - `TR` (True Range) = max(high−low, |high−prev close|, |low−prev close|) — note this is also exactly what Average True Range (next idea) needs, so the two should share one implementation.
  - `+DI`/`-DI` = smoothed(`DM`/`TR`) × 100 (13-day smoothing is his default), each always positive.
  - `DX` = `|+DI − −DI| / (+DI + −DI) × 100`; `ADX` = smoothed `DX` (e.g. 13-day EMA).
  - Rules: trade long only when `+DI13 > -DI13`, short only when the reverse; only trust a trend-following approach while ADX is *rising* — a falling ADX means increasing whipsaw risk; ADX below both DI lines marks a "lull" (the longer it stays there, the stronger the eventual move) — **when ADX rises 4 steps from its low point (e.g. 9→13), that specifically "rings a bell" on a new trend being born**, a concrete, checkable early-trend-detection trigger; ADX rallying above both DI lines = overheated, and a downturn from there is a take-partial-profits signal, not a reversal signal.
  - Natural fit: could become the actual mechanism behind the trend/range `regime` field the earlier idea proposes, rather than (or alongside) the EMA-new-high/low heuristic.

- **Missing entirely: Average True Range (ATR).** Same ch. 24 section (pp. 93-94), and referenced again in ch. 41's multi-ATR channel discussion (already logged above). `TR` as defined just above, `ATR` = its N-day average (13 is his default). Concrete, numeric usage rules he gives, none of which this app currently has any equivalent for:
  - **Entries**: pullbacks within an uptrend tend to bottom out near −1 ATR from the EMA — a specific, checkable "how deep is deep enough" answer that the current `evaluate_wave`/Stochastic-oversold logic doesn't give (it only asks *whether* there's a pullback, never *how far* is a reasonable entry).
  - **Stops**: should be **at least 1 ATR** from entry — closer than that sits inside normal market noise and will get stopped out by randomness, not a real reversal. This is a concrete lower bound the current stop-loss formula (swing-low minus a volatility buffer built from short-EMA downside penetrations) doesn't explicitly enforce or cross-check against.
  - **Profit targets**: take a third of the position off at +1 ATR, another third at +2 ATR, the rest at +3 ATR. This app doesn't compute profit targets at all today (only signal + stop) — ATR gives a ready-made, well-specified way to add them.
  - Trading outside ±3 ATR is rare/extreme and tends to mean-revert — a cheap "this move looks overextended" flag.

- **Refinement to an existing mechanism: adaptive (percentile-based) overbought/oversold thresholds instead of fixed 30/70.** Raised independently for both Stochastic (ch. 25, p. 95, and ch. 26, p. 99) and RSI (ch. 27, p. 101) — stated as a general rule, not tied to one oscillator: *"Place those lines so that an oscillator spends only about 5 percent of its time beyond each line [over the past six months]. Readjust these lines once every three months."* Explicitly: *"Overbought and oversold RSI levels vary from market to market and even from year to year in the same market. There are no magical levels for all tops and bottoms... oversold and overbought signals are like hot and cold readings on a window thermometer. The same temperature levels mean different things in summer or winter."*
  - **This directly explains something from earlier in this conversation**: `STOCHASTIC_OVERSOLD = 30.0` in `backend/app/signals/triple_screen.py` is a single fixed constant applied to every ticker — which is exactly why ORCL's %K only dipped below 30 on 10 days across two years of its 2023-2025 rally (see the earlier ORCL backtest discussion). Elder's own recommendation is that 30 was never meant to be universal — each stock should get its own threshold, recalibrated quarterly, set so it's breached only ~5% of the time over the trailing 6 months. A high-momentum stock like ORCL during that stretch would get a *less extreme* (higher) oversold threshold than a fixed 30, and would very plausibly have generated more (or earlier) qualifying pullback signals.
  - Implementation shape: replace the fixed `STOCHASTIC_OVERSOLD`/`STOCHASTIC_OVERBOUGHT` constants with a per-ticker, rolling-6-month 5th/95th-percentile calculation (or similar), recomputed roughly quarterly per the book's own cadence. A real product decision needed here too: recalibrating thresholds per-stock makes cross-ticker signal comparison less apples-to-apples (a "30" on one ticker isn't a "30" on another anymore) — worth weighing before committing.

- **New oscillator, not currently implemented: Relative Strength Index (RSI).** Ch. 27 (pp. 99-102). This app currently has Stochastic, Force Index, and Elder-Ray as its oscillators/momentum tools — no RSI anywhere. `RSI = 100 − 100/(1 + RS)`, `RS` = average of net up-closes ÷ average of net down-closes over N days (7-9 days sharpens signals, per the book). Selling points over what's already implemented: RSI is closing-price-only so "less noisy than Stochastic" (his own side-by-side CVX chart shows RSI signals emerging earlier than Stochastic's on the same data), and RSI's own trendlines (drawn on the RSI series itself, not price) tend to break 1-2 days *ahead* of the actual price trendline break — a distinct early-warning mechanism nothing in this app currently has. Gets the identical divergence treatment as Stochastic (see the divergence idea above) and the same adaptive-threshold treatment as the idea directly above. Lowest-priority of this batch — it's additive/confirmatory rather than filling a structural gap, and the app's Elder-Ray + Stochastic + Force Index combination already covers the "oscillator" role Triple Screen's Screen 2 needs.

- **Missing volume-trend-confirmation rules, separate from Force Index.** Ch. 28 "Volume" (pp. 103-107). This app has Force Index (volume × price-change, already implemented), but Elder treats plain volume-vs-its-own-average as its own independent confirming signal, not something Force Index fully subsumes: rising volume on a new price high/low confirms the move is likely to be retested or exceeded; **shrinking volume while a trend continues means the trend is "ripe for reversal"** (most reliable at tops — "it takes buying to put prices up, but they can fall of their own weight," less reliable at bottoms, since declines can persist on thin volume); shrinking volume during a *countertrend pullback* within a larger trend signals the pullback is nearly spent (a buy-the-dip timing cue, distinct from what Wave/Force Index already check); a **standardized threshold**: "high volume" = ≥25% above the trailing 2-week average, "low volume" = ≥25% below. Worth checking whether `score_volume_confirmation` (confidence scoring) already captures any of this or only checks the single trigger-bar's volume against a 20-day average — if the latter, this is a real, additive gap (trend-relative volume behavior over the *whole move*, not just one bar).

- **Two new volume-based indicators, both missing entirely: On-Balance Volume (OBV) and Accumulation/Distribution (A/D).** Ch. 29 (pp. 107-112), developed by Joseph Granville and Larry Williams respectively — grep confirms neither exists anywhere in `app/`.
  - **OBV**: a running total — add the day's volume if close > prior close, subtract if close < prior close, unchanged if flat. Simple, cheap to compute from data already fetched. Gets the standard divergence treatment (new price high with a lower OBV high = bearish; new price low with a higher OBV low = bullish), plus a distinct use case Elder highlights: **in a trading range, an OBV breakout to a new high/low ahead of a price breakout is itself a buy/sell signal** — i.e. OBV can lead price out of a range, which ties directly into both the true/false-breakout idea and the trend/range regime idea already logged above.
  - **A/D**: more finely calibrated than OBV — instead of crediting the whole day's volume to whichever side "won," it credits volume proportional to where the close landed within the day's range: `A/D = (Close − Open) / (High − Low) × Volume`, cumulative running total. Same divergence treatment. Conceptually close to Elder-Ray (already implemented) in that both use the open/close-vs-range relationship, but A/D is cumulative and volume-weighted where Elder-Ray isn't — genuinely distinct, not a duplicate.
  - Both would need a swing-high/swing-low detector to do divergence detection properly, same prerequisite noted in the MACD-H/Stochastic/RSI divergence idea above — worth building that once, shared across all of them, rather than four separate implementations.

- **Additional Force Index trading rules found in ch. 30 (pp. 112-116) beyond what's already implemented.** The app already has Force Index (2-EMA for entries, 13-EMA for trend confirmation) matching `docs/Analyse.md`, but the book has several more specific rules not currently in the codebase:
  - **Spike magnitude is explicitly quantified as "5 times or more its usual depth"** for the 2-day EMA down-spike-then-recoil rally signal — a different, much simpler definition than this app's existing `_is_force_index_spike` (1.0 × trailing-13-bar standard deviation, a `decisions`-recorded choice since the book "gives no numeric definition of spike" for the *Screen 2* usage specifically). This chapter's "5x usual depth" is a distinct, additional signal from a different part of the book (short-term reversal timing, not Screen 2's oversold-pullback classification) — worth deciding whether it's a second, separate check or an argument for revisiting the existing stdev-based threshold.
  - **Explicit directional asymmetry, stated plainly**: "this signal doesn't work well in uptrends — markets recoil from down spikes but not from up spikes... spikes that point down reflect intense fear, which doesn't persist for very long. Spikes that point up reflect excessive enthusiasm and greed, which can persist for quite a long time." The app's `_is_force_index_spike` currently treats both directions symmetrically (same 1-stdev test for `negative=True` and `negative=False`). This is a genuine, well-justified methodology point worth a real decision: should the BEARISH/overbought-rally spike condition use a stricter threshold than the BULLISH/oversold-pullback one, given Elder's own claim they aren't equally reliable?
  - **13-day Force Index divergence has the same explicit centerline-crossing requirement** as MACD-Histogram's divergence ("for a divergence to be legitimate, this indicator must make a new peak, then fall below its zero line, and then rise above that line again, but tracing a lower peak... If there is no crossover, then there is no legitimate divergence.") — a third instance of the same rule, reinforcing that it's a real, consistent requirement across indicators, not MACD-H-specific.
  - Pyramiding: add to an existing long each time the 2-day EMA dips negative again during a continued uptrend (not just a single entry) — this app has no concept of adding to an existing position at all today, worth noting as out of scope for a signal-only app unless position-sizing/pyramiding becomes a real feature.

- **New framework: "Indicator Seasons."** Ch. 32 "Time" (pp. 122-124) — a four-way classification of any oscillator's state by combining its **slope** with its **position relative to centerline**, applied here to MACD-Histogram (already computed in this app) but stated as general-purpose ("we can apply the concept of seasons to most indicators and timeframes"):

    | Slope | vs. Centerline | Season | Action |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Rising | Below | Spring | Best time to go long |
    | Rising | Above | Summer | Uptrend recognized by crowd; profit-take longs into strength |
    | Falling | Above | Autumn | Best time to go short |
    | Falling | Below | Winter | Downtrend recognized by crowd; cover shorts into weakness |

    The insight: Spring/Autumn (the *early*, still-below/above-centerline-crossing-back state) are explicitly called the best entries, precisely because they're emotionally hard to act on — "memories of the downtrend are still fresh" in Spring, so few traders buy even though it's the best risk/reward entry. This is directly computable from the existing `macd_histogram` series (slope check already exists for Impulse; centerline/sign check is trivial) and gives a more granular 4-state read than the current 3-state Impulse (Green/Red/Blue) or 3-state Tide (BULLISH/BEARISH/NEUTRAL) — could be a purely informational addition to the analysis response (a "market season" label) without touching signal logic at all, which makes it a relatively low-risk one to add.

- **Performance-grading rubric, to develop further once ch. 55 "Is This an A-Trade?" is read** — ch. 33 (p. 131) previews it: grade a completed trade by what percentage of the price channel's height it captured (channel = the % envelope around the EMA from ch. 22/41, already logged above). Captured ≥30% of the channel height = an "A" trade, ~10% = a "C" trade. Noted here as a placeholder; will be expanded with the full rubric when ch. 55 is read, since duplicating it now would be premature given ch. 33 only previews the concept in a day-trading aside.

- **Missing entirely: broad-market-breadth context for individual-stock signals.** Ch. 34-36 (pp. 133-142) — New High-New Low Index (NH-NL), % of Stocks above their 50-day MA, and the Advance/Decline line. Elder's own justification is directly relevant to a single-stock signal app, not just index trading: *"general market trends are responsible for as much as half the movement in individual stocks."* None of these are currently computed anywhere in this app (it only ever analyzes the specific tickers a user is watching/holding, never "the market" as a whole).
  - **NH-NL** = count of tickers hitting a new 52-week high minus count hitting a new 52-week low, across a broad universe, trended and compared against a centerline (extra windows: 20-day and 65-day look-backs give more sensitive, shorter-term versions; specific extreme thresholds given for the weekly version — below −4,000 then rallying back above it is called a "Spike" buy signal, above +2,500 confirms a bull market).
  - **Stocks above 50-day MA**: % of a broad universe trading above their own 50-day MA, with 75%/25% as typical (not absolute) reversal-zone reference lines — signals fire on the *reversal away from* an extreme, not the extreme itself.
  - **Advance/Decline line**: cumulative daily (advancers − decliners) across an exchange; same divergence treatment as every other indicator above.
  - **What this actually needs, and where to get it**: all three need a broad ticker *universe* (the list of which tickers to check for new highs/lows/above-MA today) — this app currently only fetches data for tickers a user has explicitly added, nothing broader. That list is the only missing piece; once you have it, fetching daily OHLCV for every name on it reuses the exact same `YFinanceProvider`/`StooqProvider`/`CachedDataProvider` machinery already in place — no new *data* source for the prices themselves, just more of the same calls (cache-friendly, since the cache already keys per ticker).
    - **A free, ready-made S&P 500 constituent list**: [datasets/s-and-p-500-companies on GitHub](https://github.com/datasets/s-and-p-500-companies/blob/main/data/constituents.csv) — a maintained CSV of current constituents, sourced from Wikipedia. [datahub.io's S&P 500 dataset](https://datahub.io/core/s-and-p-500-companies-financials) mirrors the same underlying data with financials attached. Wikipedia's own ["List of S&P 500 companies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_S%26P_500_companies) page is the ultimate free source either repo scrapes — `pandas.read_html()` against it directly is a well-worn, if slightly fragile (page-structure-dependent), one-line alternative to depending on a third-party mirror staying fresh.
    - **Historical constituents** (for a proper multi-year backtest of NH-NL/breadth, since membership changes over time): [fja05680/sp500 on GitHub](https://github.com/fja05680/sp500/blob/master/S%26P%20500%20Historical%20Components%20%26%20Changes.csv) tracks additions/removals by date.
    - Smaller, cheaper universe alternative worth real consideration before committing to 500 names: the **user's own watchlist/portfolio as the counted universe** instead of the whole S&P 500 — needs zero new sourcing at all (every ticker in it is already fetched), trading breadth accuracy for zero infra cost. Worth an explicit decision between "get a real constituent list" and "just count the user's own tickers" — not because the former is unobtainable (it isn't, per above), but because they're genuinely different sizes of effort for a similar payoff.

- **Ch. 37 "Consensus and Commitment Indicators"** (pp. 142-148) — advisor/journalist sentiment polls, magazine-cover contrarian signals, CFTC Commitment-of-Traders reports, and insider-trading clusters. None of these come from OHLCV, so all four needed real sourcing research — done now, with concrete free options found for every one of them (nothing here is a dead end):
  - **Insider trading clusters** (p. 147) — **confirmed available for free, already, via the `yfinance` dependency this app already has installed.** Verified live in this session: `yf.Ticker("AAPL").insider_transactions` returns a populated DataFrame (`Shares`, `Text`, `Start Date`, `Ownership`, `Value` columns) sourced from Yahoo Finance's own insider-transactions page — real data, right now, no new vendor. This app's `DataProvider` protocol (`app/data/base.py`) just doesn't have a method for it yet — `YFinanceProvider` only ever calls `.history()`, never `.insider_transactions`. Purely a wrapper-extension task, not a sourcing problem. (The Stooq fallback has no equivalent — this field would only populate on the primary yfinance path.)
  - **Short interest / "Short Percent of Float" and "Days to Cover"** (pp. 146-148) — **also confirmed available for free via the same already-installed `yfinance` dependency.** Verified live: `yf.Ticker("AAPL").info` returns `sharesShort`, `shortRatio`, `shortPercentOfFloat`, `sharesShortPriorMonth`, `floatShares`, all populated with real current numbers. Same situation as above: not a missing data source, just an unwrapped one — `DataProvider` would need a new method (e.g. `get_short_interest(ticker)`) alongside the existing two.
  - **Advisor/journalist sentiment consensus** (the book's own named services — Investors Intelligence, MarketVane — are paid subscription services with no free public feed found) — the closest genuinely free, modern equivalent doing the same job: **the AAII Investor Sentiment Survey** ([aaii.com/sentimentsurvey](https://www.aaii.com/sentimentsurvey)), a weekly bulls/neutral/bears poll of individual investors, published free every Thursday and running continuously since 1987 — one of the longest-running free sentiment datasets that exists. This is a real, sourceable alternative to the book's specific (paid) services, not a dead end.
  - **Magazine-cover contrarian signal** — inherently qualitative (literally "count how many bullish magazine covers appeared this month"), so there's no clean structured feed for the *literal* technique. The closest modern, structured, freely-available proxy for "how bullish is financial journalism right now": **Alpha Vantage's News Sentiment API** — already free-tier accessible (25 requests/day), runs NLP-based bullish/bearish scoring against financial news articles, filterable by ticker. `docs/Analyse.md` §9 already lists Alpha Vantage as a considered-but-not-adopted data provider for plain OHLCV (rate-limited too tightly for that use), but its sentiment endpoint is a different product with a separate, workable-for-this-purpose free quota — worth reconsidering Alpha Vantage specifically for this narrower use case even if it's still wrong for bulk price data.
  - **CFTC Commitment of Traders** (futures-only, so not directly usable by this stocks-only app's own tickers, but sourceable for completeness and in case that scope ever changes) — **fully free and public**, published weekly by the CFTC itself: [cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders/index.htm) for the official reports, or the [`cot_reports` Python package on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/cot-reports/) which wraps CFTC's bulk historical files into a ready-to-use DataFrame. No sourcing obstacle at all here — the only real blocker is that it's futures/options-only by definition, which is a product-scope mismatch, not a data-availability one.
  - **Bonus, not in the book's own ch. 37 list but serves the identical contrarian-sentiment purpose and is genuinely free and stock-market-relevant** (unlike CFTC): the **CBOE Put/Call Ratio** — [cboe.com/data/putcallratio.aspx](https://www.cboe.com/data/putcallratio.aspx) publishes free historical put/call ratio data directly from the exchange that actually lists the options. Worth adding to the sourcing list even though Elder's own ch. 37 doesn't name it, since it fills the same "extreme reading = contrarian signal" role his other consensus indicators do, with a cleaner free download than most of them.

- **Part Eight, "Trading Vehicles" (ch. 42-47, pp. 175-197), reviewed** — ch. 42 "Stocks" (the one vehicle this app actually handles) has no new technical content, just a preview of a weekend divergence-scanning workflow that ch. 56 "Scanning for Possible Trades" (not yet read) covers properly — noted here to avoid duplicating when that chapter is read. Ch. 44 Options, 45 CFDs, 46 Futures, 47 Forex are entirely vehicle-specific mechanics (margin, greeks, contract specs, pip values, rollover) — confirmed via grep with no transferable technical-analysis content beyond generic references to tools already covered elsewhere; genuinely not applicable to this stocks-only app, not just unimplemented.
  - One real caution worth keeping from ch. 43 "ETFs" (pp. 176-178), though: leveraged, inverse, and commodity/volatility ETFs frequently **fail to track their underlying index** over any but the shortest holding periods — his own examples: a volatility ETF (VXX) lost 90% of its value while the $VIX it's supposed to track stayed range-bound; a natural gas ETF (UNG) fell from $500 to under $20 while the actual natural gas spot price didn't remotely do that. If a user ever adds one of these to their watchlist/portfolio, this app's Elder Triple Screen signals — built and validated for ordinary equities — would be analyzing a vehicle with structural decay unrelated to real trend/momentum, with no warning anywhere today. Low priority/speculative (this app is documented as stock-focused, and detecting "is this ticker a leveraged/inverse ETF" reliably from ticker metadata alone isn't trivial), but worth a note rather than silently assuming it can never come up.

- **Missing entirely: profit targets.** Ch. 53 "How to Set Profit Targets" (pp. 215-219). This app computes a signal, a confidence score, and a protective stop — but never a target price. Three concrete, style-dependent target techniques, all buildable from things already computed or already logged above: **swing trades** — exit in the "value zone" between the fast/slow EMA, or at the near channel line (channel idea already logged); **day-trades** (not this app's use case, skip) — exit at the first sign of an opposing oscillator divergence; **position/long-term trades** — target the nearest prior support/resistance level above (long) or below (short) current price (S/R idea already logged). Explicit, checkable sanity rule to pair with it: **potential reward should be at least 2x the risk** (distance to target ÷ distance to stop ≥ 2) — "it seldom pays to risk a dollar to make a dollar." Could be surfaced as a new field alongside the existing stop, with a reward:risk ratio that factors into (or at least is displayed next to) confidence. **Update from ch. 58's own Tradebill (p. 242)**: he gives one specific numeric formula for what he calls an "A" target: entry price + 30% of that day's channel height — a direct, checkable number, consistent with the A-trade grading rubric logged below (≥30% of channel height captured = an A-trade), so the target and the grading scale share the same 30% figure by design.

- **Verified, concrete discrepancy from the book's own named source: `protective_stop()`'s SafeZone implementation is missing its multiplier coefficient.** Ch. 54 "How to Set Stops" (pp. 220-221) gives SafeZone's exact original formula, and `backend/app/portfolio/risk.py`'s `protective_stop()` docstring explicitly cites "SafeZone concept" as its own basis — so this is a direct, checkable comparison, not a vague gap:
  - **Book's formula**: trend direction from a **22-day** EMA slope; average the downside penetrations of that EMA over a 10-20 day lookback (Average Downside Penetration); **multiply that average by a coefficient of 2 or greater**, then subtract from price. His own words: *"placing your stop any closer would be self-defeating."* For a short (uptrend reversed to down), the coefficient starts at **3**, not 2 — explicitly wider, because "shorting near the highs requires wider stops than buying near quiet, sold-out bottoms."
  - **Current code** (`app/portfolio/risk.py:38-108`): `_SWING_LOW_WINDOW_DAYS = 10` (fine, inside the book's 10-20 day range) but `_VOLATILITY_EMA_PERIOD = 13`, not 22 (a defensible choice — 13 is already this app's standard fast-EMA period, reused elsewhere for Elder-Ray/Impulse, so there's a real coherence argument for it — but it is a deviation from SafeZone's own specific number, not the same thing with a different name). More importantly: the formula is `swing_low - volatility_buffer`, where `volatility_buffer` is the **raw, unmultiplied** average downside penetration — there's no ×2 (or ×3) coefficient anywhere in the calculation. Per the book's own explicit warning, this means the app's stops are very plausibly placed inside the zone of normal market noise, closer to price than SafeZone's own inventor considers safe.
  - This is a `decisions`-worthy question for whoever picks this up: was omitting the coefficient itself a deliberate simplification (if so, it isn't recorded as one anywhere findable), or is this a genuine correctness gap against the app's own cited methodology? Either way it's now precisely specified enough to resolve one way or the other.

- **New stop-placement technique: "Nic's stop"** (ch. 54, pp. 221-222), a named alternative Elder actually prefers in his own worked examples. Instead of placing a stop just below the single most recent swing low (which is exactly where `protective_stop()` anchors today — `window["low"].min()`), place it just below the **second**-lowest low in the lookback window (mirror: second-highest high for a short). Rationale: everyone else's stops cluster right below/above the single obvious extreme, so price tends to run there first before reversing — a stop at the second-lowest low gets you out with less slippage and a smaller loss than waiting for the crowd's cluster to get hit. Cheap to implement (just needs the 2nd-order statistic instead of the min over the same window already being read), and could be an alternative stop-calculation mode or a documented reason to move the existing anchor. Related, much smaller detail also from this section: avoid placing a computed stop at a round number (nudge a few cents away) since round numbers attract clustering — very low priority, a polish item at best.

- **New feature: progressive profit-protection ratchet ("cuffing the trade").** Ch. 54, pp. 223-224. A dynamic stop-tightening scheme, structurally different from (and complementary to) the static SafeZone-style stop: once open profit on a position reaches a pre-planned checkpoint (his example: 30% of the profit target), move the stop to breakeven; as profit grows further, progressively lock in a growing fraction of the open profit (his example: protect 1/3 of open profit past breakeven, presumably increasing further as the trade extends, though he doesn't give a second numeric checkpoint). Rationale: "as a trade moves in your favor, your remaining potential gain begins to shrink, while your risk... keeps increasing" — the reward:risk ratio decays as a winner ages, so the stop needs to tighten to compensate. This app already tracks `position.entry_price`/quantity (`PositionORM`), so the prerequisite data exists; the missing piece is entirely the ratchet logic itself, which doesn't exist in any form today (the current stop is recomputed fresh from price action each time, never anchored to how far into profit the specific position already is). A genuinely new, sizable idea — worth a real product decision on the checkpoint percentages before scoping.
  - Related, smaller discipline rule from the same section worth a one-line UX note if this app ever exposes manual stop editing: **never loosen a stop** — only ever let it move in the trade's favor. If a "set custom stop" feature is ever added, it should probably warn or block a move that widens risk versus the previously computed value.

- **New feature: post-trade performance grading ("Is this an A-trade?").** Ch. 55 (pp. 225-230) gives three exact, checkable formulas for grading a *completed* trade — none of which this app can compute today because (per the 6% Rule gap logged above) there's no closed-trade/trade-history table at all:
    - `Buy grade = (day's high − buy price) / (day's high − day's low)` — how close to the entry day's low you actually bought; >50% is "very good."
    - `Sell grade = (sell price − day's low) / (day's high − day's low)` — how close to the exit day's high you actually sold; >50% is "very good."
    - `Trade grade = (sell price − buy price) / (channel high − channel low, measured on entry day)` — the trade's actual gain as a fraction of the day's channel height (the % envelope from ch. 22/41, already logged) — a **≥30% capture is an "A" trade**, ~10% a "C" trade. This is explicitly preferred over grading by raw dollars/percent-return alone, since it accounts for how much was actually realistically available to capture that day.
  - All three are cheap, pure-function calculations once a trade-history table exists (entry price+date, exit price+date, plus that day's OHLC and channel bounds, all of which come from data this app already fetches) — this pairs naturally with the trade-history-table idea from the 6% Rule gap: building that table once would unlock both the accurate 6% Rule *and* this grading feature together, rather than as two separate efforts.
  - A related, bigger and more product-shaped idea from the same chapter, previewed here and left for a `docs/tasks` scoping decision rather than full elaboration: Elder has the user **name and register their own trading strategies** (e.g. "false breakout + divergence," "pullback to value") and tags every trade against one, tracking which strategies actually perform well for them over time, asking "is this an A-trade [for one of MY named strategies]?" before ever entering one. This is a real, distinct feature — closer to a personal trading journal/strategy tracker than a signal-engine change — and probably belongs bundled with the ch. 57-59 "Record-Keeping" ideas once those are read, rather than scoped alone.

- **Scanning workflow (ch. 56, pp. 230-232) — ties together two ideas already logged rather than introducing a wholly new one.** Elder's actual weekend workflow: run a semiautomatic MACD-divergence scanner (flags stocks that have completed *parts* of a potential divergence, not full ones — "not an automatic trade finder... a watchdog") across a broad universe (S&P 500), then **compare the size of the bullish-candidate list against the bearish-candidate list as a market-mood gauge** — his own example: 4-5 bullish candidates vs. 70-80 bearish ones told him the broad market was "perched at the edge of a cliff." This is a cheaper, complementary mechanism to the NH-NL/breadth idea logged earlier: instead of needing a broad-universe data feed, this app could run its own (once-built) divergence detector across just the user's own watchlist/portfolio tickers and count how many are showing bullish- vs. bearish-divergence setups right now, as a second lightweight "personal breadth" signal alongside the earlier-proposed Tide-direction count. Also notes a practical filter worth keeping in mind if a broader scan/candidate-discovery feature is ever built: exclude very low-average-volume stocks from scans (his own cutoff: under 0.5-1M average daily shares) since they're noisier and slippage-prone — a minor addition if that feature ever exists, not a priority on its own.

- **New, concrete, checkable idea: earnings/dividend-date awareness.** Ch. 58 "Creating and Scoring Trade Plans" (p. 241) — the Tradebill's very first data fields, before any technical analysis, are the ticker's next earnings date and dividend date, with an explicit rationale: *"most traders avoid holding stocks whose earnings are about to be reported. A nasty earnings surprise can do serious damage to your position"* (a gap-through-the-stop risk no technical stop-loss formula can protect against, since it can jump straight past any stop level). This app has zero earnings-calendar awareness today. **Confirmed available for free, already, via the same already-installed `yfinance` dependency** — verified live in this session: `yf.Ticker("AAPL").calendar` returns `{'Dividend Date': ..., 'Ex-Dividend Date': ..., 'Earnings Date': [...], 'Earnings High/Low/Average': ..., 'Revenue High/Low/Average': ...}` with real populated dates, and `.earnings_dates` returns a full historical+upcoming DataFrame of earnings dates with estimate/actual/surprise columns. Same pattern as insider transactions and short interest above: not a missing data source, purely a `DataProvider`-protocol wrapper extension. A simple "earnings expected within N days" flag/warning on a BUY signal or an open position would be a cheap, high-value addition, independent of everything else in this list.

- **Trade Apgar — a per-strategy, veto-capable scoring pattern, structurally different from this app's confidence score.** Ch. 58 (pp. 238-241). Elder's own worked example (his "false breakout with a divergence" strategy): five yes/no/partial questions, each scored 0/1/2, summed to a max of 10; trade only if **total ≥ 7 AND no single question scored zero** — the all-or-nothing veto on any single zero is the important structural difference from a pure weighted sum. This app's existing confidence score (`docs/Analyse.md`, 5 weighted components) has no equivalent hard-veto mechanism at the *scoring* level — a catastrophically bad single component can still be outweighed by the other four rather than killing the score outright (Tide misalignment is the one component that already gates the signal structurally upstream of scoring, per `score_tide_alignment`, but nothing else does). Worth a real methodology discussion, not just an implementation task: should any other single-component failure (e.g. Impulse flatly gating the direction) hard-zero the confidence score rather than just reduce it proportionally? Separately, the bigger idea already flagged under ch. 55 — user-defined, named strategies each with their own tailored Apgar-style checklist — is the natural home for this pattern if/when multi-strategy support is ever built; this entry is about the *scoring mechanic* itself (veto-on-zero), which is worth considering even for the single existing Triple-Screen strategy, independent of that bigger multi-strategy question.

- **Trade Journal structure (ch. 59, pp. 243-247) — completes and enriches the trade-history-table idea already logged under the 6% Rule gap.** Beyond entry/exit price+date and the buy/sell/trade grades (already logged), Elder's actual journal schema adds two things worth folding into that same future table's design rather than building separately:
  - **A structured exit-reason taxonomy**, not just a raw P&L number: target hit, stop hit, reached value zone/channel edge, "going nowhere," "starting to turn," plus two explicitly-named *negative* exits — "couldn't stand the pain" (an undisciplined early exit) and "recognized junk trade after entry" (an admission the setup was never actually valid). He specifically credits reviewing the equity curve *filtered to just the "couldn't stand the pain" tag* with convincing him to never trade without stops again — i.e. the taxonomy's value is in enabling this kind of segmented self-analysis, not just labeling.
  - **A scheduled "two months later" follow-up review**: revisit every closed trade once it's no longer at the chart's right edge, mark entry/exit on the now-fully-formed chart, and write a retrospective comment on what was actually right or wrong — explicitly credited with revealing two concrete, actionable patterns in his own trading (stops placed too tight; good short-term exits that then missed larger continuations). For this app, this is a real (if speculative and UI-heavy) feature: a periodic reminder/prompt to revisit trades closed ~2 months ago with their full subsequent price history visible, prompting a short written note — much bigger in scope than the grading formulas alone, and probably belongs behind the same trade-history-table prerequisite as everything else in this cluster, worth scoping as its own step rather than bundled automatically with the base table.
  - Equity-curve segmentation by strategy tag and exit-reason tag (not just one aggregate curve) is the payoff of collecting both fields — noted here for completeness, but is really a reporting/analytics feature on top of the same underlying trade-history data, not a new data requirement of its own.

- **Ch. 57 "Your Daily Homework" (pp. 234-238), reviewed — mostly a personal pre-market ritual, not something to encode.** The macro-context checklist (overseas markets, economic calendar, key commodities/currencies, NH-NL, VIX) reinforces ideas already logged (market breadth, general awareness of broad conditions) without adding anything new and computable. The "psychological readiness self-test" (5 yes/no/so-so questions about the *trader's own* physical/mental state, mood, and schedule, scored and color-coded) is explicitly about the human's fitness to trade that day, not something derivable from market data — genuinely out of scope for a signal-computation engine, though conceivably a free-text personal note field alongside a trade plan (see the Trade Journal idea above) could let a user record their own version of this manually, if the broader journal feature is ever built.
